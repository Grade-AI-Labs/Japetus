# Research Business Case Builder

This skill builds business cases for user research — whether for a single study, a tool investment, a headcount request, or the research function itself. It translates research value into the language decision-makers actually respond to.

## Why a business case matters

Leadership doesn't oppose research because they dislike users. They oppose it because someone hasn't yet connected research to the outcomes they're accountable for. A business case makes that connection explicit — turning "we should talk to users" into "here is what we will learn, here is what we will do with it, here is what it will cost, and here is what we stand to gain."

A strong business case does four things:
1. Names the problem in business terms, not research terms
2. Explains the proposed solution and what it will produce
3. Quantifies costs and expected benefits honestly
4. Gives decision-makers everything they need to say yes

---

## The eight-part business case structure

### Part 1: Executive summary

One to three short paragraphs. Written last. Placed first. Should give a time-pressured decision-maker everything they need to understand the proposal without reading further.

**Must include:**
- What the project is and why it matters now
- The core problem it addresses
- Expected benefits and costs in plain numbers
- A clear recommendation to proceed

**Formula:**
"This business case proposes [what] to address [specific problem]. The research is expected to [primary benefit with estimate]. Total investment is [cost]. Net benefit is [amount], representing an ROI of [percentage]. This work directly supports [OKR or strategic goal]."

**Example:**
"This business case proposes a 6-week usability study on the onboarding flow to identify the barriers causing 40% of new users to drop off before completing setup. Addressing these barriers is estimated to increase 30-day retention by 10-15%, generating an additional $180,000-$270,000 annually. Total research investment is $14,000. This work directly supports our Q3 OKR to increase activation from 60% to 75%."

---

### Part 2: Problem statement and background

Define the problem the research will address. This section must be in business terms — not research terms. The problem is not "we don't have enough user insight." The problem is "activation is at 60% and our Q3 target is 75% and we don't know what's blocking the gap."

**Must include:**
- The specific business problem with supporting data
- Why this problem matters to the business now
- What happens if it goes unaddressed
- Any existing evidence pointing toward the cause

**Questions to answer:**
- What metric is underperforming, and by how much?
- What decisions can't be made without this knowledge?
- What has already been tried, and why hasn't it worked?
- What is the cost of inaction — in revenue, time, or risk?

**Example:**
"User engagement with the platform's advanced features is at 30%, well below the 60% benchmark for comparable SaaS products at our stage. This underutilization is directly linked to reduced renewal rates — customers who don't use advanced features churn at 3x the rate of power users. We have no current understanding of why engagement is low: whether it's a discoverability issue, a onboarding gap, or a perceived value problem. Without this understanding, product and marketing teams are making decisions based on assumptions, with significant risk of investing in the wrong solutions. The cost of continued misalignment is estimated at $20,000 in annual churn revenue."

---

### Part 3: Objectives and success metrics

Define exactly what the research will answer and how success will be measured — both for the research itself and for the downstream business outcome.

**Must include:**
- 2-4 specific research objectives (not questions — outcomes)
- The business metric each objective is designed to impact
- A measurable benchmark for each metric
- A timeframe for evaluation

**Format:**

| Objective | Business metric | Current state | Target | Timeframe |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Identify barriers to advanced feature adoption | Feature adoption rate | 30% | 50% | 6 months post-implementation |
| Understand why users churn before renewal | Renewal rate | 67% | 75% | 12 months post-implementation |

**The difference between a research objective and a business objective:**
- Research objective: "Understand why users don't engage with advanced features"
- Business objective: "Increase advanced feature adoption from 30% to 50%"

Both belong in the business case. The research objective is what you'll study. The business objective is why anyone should fund it.

---

### Part 4: Proposed solution

Describe what you will actually do — the research design, methods, participant profile, and timeline. Be specific enough that a non-researcher can understand what they're funding.

**Must include:**
- Research method(s) and why they're appropriate for this question
- Number of participants and how they'll be recruited
- Format (moderated/unmoderated, remote/in-person)
- What will be produced (report, recommendations, workshop, repository)
- Timeline from kick-off to delivery

**Keep it plain:** Avoid jargon. "We will conduct 8 moderated 60-minute interviews with users who have not engaged with advanced features in the past 30 days" is clearer than "We will conduct a qualitative generative research study using a semi-structured interview protocol."

**Example:**
"We propose conducting 10 moderated 45-minute usability sessions with users who activated an account in the last 90 days but have not completed the onboarding checklist. Sessions will be conducted remotely via Zoom and recorded with participant consent. We will observe users attempting key tasks, ask them to think aloud, and probe on moments of hesitation or confusion. Output: a research report with prioritized usability findings, a set of actionable recommendations, and a 30-minute readout with the product and design teams. Timeline: 6 weeks from approval."

---

### Part 5: Resource requirements

Break down everything needed to execute the research. Decision-makers need to see that you've thought through the full cost — not just the headline number.

**Categories to cover:**

**People (time, not just titles):**
- Researcher: X hours for planning, recruiting, facilitating, synthesizing, reporting
- PM or designer: X hours for alignment, review, and implementation
- Any external support: agency, freelancer, participant panel

**Budget:**
- Participant incentives (number of participants × incentive per person)
- Research tool subscriptions (usability testing platform, survey tool, repository)
- Recruiting costs (panel access, agency fees, screener tool)
- Any training or onboarding

**Technology:**
- Recording and transcription tools
- Analysis tools (Dovetail, Miro, Atlas.ti)
- Video conferencing

**Dependencies:**
- Stakeholder availability for kick-off, debrief, and readout
- Access to analytics data or product instrumentation for baseline metrics
- Legal review for consent forms if required

**Example budget table:**

| Item | Cost |
|---|---|
| Participant incentives (10 × $75) | $750 |
| Usability testing platform (1 month) | $300 |
| Transcription service (10 sessions) | $200 |
| Researcher time (40 hrs × $150/hr internal rate) | $6,000 |
| PM / design review time (8 hrs × $150/hr) | $1,200 |
| **Total** | **$8,450** |

---

### Part 6: Expected benefits and business impact

Quantify what the research is expected to produce — and be honest about the chain of causation. Research doesn't directly increase revenue; it produces insights that drive changes that improve metrics that impact revenue.

**The impact chain:**
Research findings → Product or process changes → Metric improvement → Business outcome

Make each link explicit.

**Direct benefits (can be estimated in dollars):**
- Retention improvement: (increase in retention rate) × (annual customer value) × (number of customers)
- Conversion improvement: (increase in conversion rate) × (average customer value) × (monthly visitors)
- Support cost reduction: (decrease in support tickets) × (cost per ticket)
- Development cost avoided: (features not built) × (average feature development cost)

**Indirect benefits (quantify where possible, name where not):**
- Faster decision-making (fewer debates, earlier alignment)
- Reduced redesign risk (catching problems before they ship)
- Stakeholder confidence and research culture
- Team time saved from guessing vs. knowing

**Honesty principle:** Use conservative estimates. A 5% retention improvement is more credible than a 20% claim. Present a range where appropriate. State your assumptions clearly.

**Example:**
"If this research identifies and resolves the primary onboarding barriers, we estimate a 10% improvement in 30-day retention (conservative, based on industry benchmarks for usability improvements of this type). At our current volume of 3,000 new activations per month and an average annual customer value of $600, this represents an additional $180,000 in annual revenue. Even at a 5% improvement, the benefit ($90,000) significantly exceeds the research investment ($8,450)."

---

### Part 7: Cost-benefit analysis

Present the numbers cleanly so the decision-maker can see the ROI at a glance.

**Formula:**
- Net benefit = Total quantified benefits − Total costs
- ROI = (Net benefit ÷ Total costs) × 100

**Example:**

| | |
|---|---|
| Total research investment | $8,450 |
| Conservative annual benefit (10% retention improvement) | $180,000 |
| **Net benefit** | **$171,550** |
| **ROI** | **>2,000%** |

When the ROI is very high (which it usually is for research — the cost of a study is almost always small compared to the cost of the problem), present the conservative case alongside it to establish credibility: "Even if the impact is 50% smaller than estimated, the return on investment exceeds 1,000%."

**If benefits can't be quantified:** Use risk mitigation framing. "This research will cost $8,450. The feature we're evaluating costs $250,000 to build. If research prevents one significant redesign in the next 12 months, it pays for itself 30 times over."

---

### Part 8: Risk assessment and mitigation

Name the risks honestly. Decision-makers trust proposals that acknowledge what could go wrong.

**Common research risks and mitigations:**

| Risk | Likelihood | Mitigation | Contingency |
|---|---|---|---|
| Low participant recruitment rate | Medium | Over-recruit by 20%; use professional panel as backup | Extend timeline by 1 week; adjust screener criteria |
| Findings don't surface clear direction | Low | Pilot session with 2 participants before full study to validate questions | Run targeted follow-up survey to validate qualitative patterns |
| Implementation delayed post-research | High | Align with PM before study begins on sprint commitment; present findings in sprint planning | Schedule follow-up check-in 30 days after readout to track action |
| Stakeholders don't act on findings | Medium | Co-create research questions with stakeholders; hold activation workshop at conclusion | Escalate to leadership with impact framing if roadmap decisions are at risk |

---

### Part 9: Implementation plan

Show the work — the phases, milestones, owners, and timeline. This signals that the proposal is ready to execute, not just ready to approve.

**Standard research project phases:**

**Phase 1: Planning (Week 1-2)**
- Stakeholder alignment meeting — confirm research questions and success criteria
- Finalize screener and recruit participants
- Develop discussion guide or test protocol
- Secure tool access and consent form approval

**Phase 2: Execution (Week 3-4)**
- Conduct research sessions
- Per-session debriefs with core team
- Ongoing synthesis after every 3-5 sessions

**Phase 3: Synthesis and reporting (Week 5)**
- Full affinity diagram and pattern identification
- Insight writing with consequence and recommendations
- Draft report and stakeholder review

**Phase 4: Activation (Week 6)**
- Formal readout with decision-makers
- Ideation or activation workshop with product and design
- Action items assigned with owners and dates

**Phase 5: Follow-up (Month 2-6)**
- 30-day check-in on implementation progress
- 3-month and 6-month metric evaluation
- Impact tracker update with actual outcomes

---

### Part 10: Performance monitoring

How will you know if it worked? Define the evaluation plan before the project begins so there's no ambiguity about success later.

**Monitoring framework:**

| Metric | Baseline | Target | Measurement method | Review timing |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 30-day retention | 60% | 70% | Analytics platform | 3 months post-launch |
| Advanced feature adoption | 30% | 50% | Product instrumentation | 6 months post-launch |
| Support tickets (onboarding-related) | 120/month | 80/month | Zendesk | 3 months post-launch |

Set calendar reminders at 30 days, 3 months, and 6 months from when research-driven changes go live. Update the impact tracker with actual results and share them back with the stakeholders who approved the work.

## Business cases for specific situations

### Justifying a single research study

Focus on: the specific decision being made, the cost of making that decision without data, and the conservative benefit estimate.

Lead with: "The product team is deciding whether to [X]. Making this decision without user evidence carries a risk of [Y]. This study costs [Z] and will resolve the question within [timeline]."

### Justifying a research tool or platform

Focus on: time savings, consistency of process, and the internal capacity it unlocks.

Quantify: researcher hours currently spent on manual tasks × hourly rate × frequency. Compare to tool cost. Add researcher capacity freed for higher-value work.

### Justifying a research headcount

Focus on: current research demand vs. supply gap, cost of under-researched decisions, and the multiplier effect of a researcher on team output.

Key data points: how many research requests are currently going unmet, how many decisions are being made without data, what the cost of one major product misstep has been in the past year.

### Justifying the research function to skeptical leadership

Lead with the pirate metrics connection: "User research directly impacts acquisition, activation, and retention — the three metrics that determine whether we grow or churn. Here is the specific connection between each research project this quarter and the metric it was designed to move."

2. **Executive summary only** — given a research proposal, write a concise 2-3 paragraph executive summary for leadership review
3. **Cost-benefit analysis** — given a research investment and expected outcomes, calculate net benefit and ROI using the appropriate formula
4. **Problem statement** — given a business situation, write a compelling problem statement that frames the research need in business terms
5. **Risk table** — given a research project, identify the most likely risks and write the mitigation and contingency plan for each

## Researcher reminders

- Write the executive summary last and place it first — it's the most important section and only makes sense after everything else is written
- The problem statement must be in business terms — "we don't have enough user insight" is not a business problem; "activation is 40% below target and we don't know why" is
- Use conservative estimates throughout — a modest claim that holds up is worth more than an ambitious one that gets challenged
- The impact chain must be explicit: research findings → product changes → metric improvement → business outcome; don't skip steps
- Quantify benefits even when it's uncomfortable — "we can't know for sure" is not a reason to omit numbers; it's a reason to present a range with stated assumptions
- Risk assessment builds credibility — proposals that name risks are trusted more than ones that don't
- The implementation plan signals readiness — decision-makers are more likely to approve work that shows it's already been thought through
- Always close with monitoring: how will we know it worked, and when will we check? Without this, there's no accountability and no proof of impact
- A business case is not a research plan — the audience is decision-makers, not researchers; every section should answer "why should we fund this?" not "how will we do this?"
