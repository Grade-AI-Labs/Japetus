# Research Shareout

This skill designs engaging, actionable research share-outs — from presentation structure to creative formats that make stakeholders care about and act on findings.

## The core principle

It doesn't matter how good the findings are if you can't communicate them effectively. A research report that sits in a folder helps nobody — not the team, not the users, and not the researcher's credibility.

The goal of every share-out: **Informative + Actionable + Fun = a presentation people remember and act on.**

Insights need to be infused with excitement and urgency to promote action. That is your job as the researcher.

---

## The audience-first rule

Before deciding format, know who's in the room and what they care about.

**For executives and C-suite:**
- Lead with business impact: revenue, conversion rate, NPS, retention, churn risk
- Give them a TL;DR slide they can understand in a glance
- Keep the main presentation to 20-30 minutes maximum
- Provide links to deeper materials for those who want more

**For product managers:**
- Connect findings to roadmap decisions and feature prioritization
- Give concrete, pointed recommendations — not just problems
- Show how insights relate to current sprint or quarter goals

**For designers:**
- Lead with video clips and behavioral observations
- Focus on friction points, task failure moments, and user expectations
- Give specific design direction as recommendations

**For engineers:**
- Include specific bugs or technical issues that surfaced in research
- Frame findings as requirements, not just observations
- Show scope and severity of issues

**For marketing:**
- Highlight language users actually use (vs. marketing language)
- Note content gaps and messaging misalignments
- Surface emotional drivers and mental models

**The CTO story:** One CTO was completely uninterested in qualitative research — until a design change recommendation from a usability study caused conversion to jump 17%. After that, he never missed a share-out. When you speak directly to a person's interests, you capture and keep their attention.

## The standard presentation formula

For any research share-out, use this structure:

### Slide 1: TL;DR / High-level overview
Executives love this. Put it first. Include:
- Top 3-5 insights (with video clip links embedded)
- The headline recommendation for each
- Any critical bugs or issues requiring immediate attention

This slide is designed to be understood in 60 seconds. If a stakeholder reads only this slide, they should know what matters and what to do about it.

### Slides 2+: The findings
- Lead with video clips — show, don't tell
- Minimal text per slide; let the videos carry the weight
- After each clip, briefly explain context and recommendation
- Use quantitative data to supplement qualitative where available (e.g., "5/7 users failed this task; UserFlow shows a 40% drop-off at this step")

### Second-to-last slide: Recommendations
For every problem or issue, include a pointed recommendation — even if it feels obvious. Teams need a starting point. The recommendation doesn't have to be implemented exactly as described; it just gets people moving.

Format: **Problem → Recommendation → Expected outcome**

### Last slide: Next steps
Include:
- When you'll meet with each team about the research
- How findings will be incorporated into the roadmap or backlog
- Upcoming research projects and how to submit requests
- Links to the full report and video library for anyone who wants to go deeper

## Making insights compelling

The difference between an insight stakeholders ignore and one they act on is specificity and consequence.

**Flat:** "80% of users couldn't finish the task."

**Compelling:** "Users started filling out the form, but when they finally got to the submit button — after an average of 3.5 minutes — 80% of users were unable to find it. This confusion caused visible frustration and anxiety, and 65% said they would give up and try a different app."

The second version puts the stakeholder in the user's shoes. It makes the problem real.

**Rules for compelling insights:**
- Name the specific moment of failure, not just the failure
- Include the emotional experience, not just the behavior
- Quantify when you can (time on task, drop-off rate, frequency of occurrence)
- Show the consequence: what happens to the user because of this problem?
- Use video clips wherever possible — watching a user rage-click a broken button lands harder than any statistic

---

## The visual-first rule

Most slides in a research presentation should contain:
- A video clip, screenshot, or user photo
- One sentence of context
- One recommendation

Not: bullets, walls of text, or data tables.

The job of the slide is to direct attention to the user's experience, not to demonstrate how much data was collected. Jaws drop and work gets done when people watch users struggle with something a team thought was obvious.

## 10 creative share-out formats

Use these when a standard presentation won't land, when stakeholders need deeper engagement, or when you want research to feel like an event rather than a meeting.

---

### 1. Usability Bingo
**Goal:** Keep stakeholders engaged and observant during usability sessions.
**When:** During any observed usability test where stakeholders tend to disengage.

**Setup:**
- Create Bingo cards with common usability issues relevant to your product (e.g., "user hesitates before clicking," "user struggles with navigation," "user reads instructions aloud")
- Distribute cards to stakeholders before sessions begin
- Stakeholders mark off issues they observe in real time
- First to complete a row wins a small prize
- Debrief using the completed Bingo cards as a discussion anchor

The act of watching for specific patterns keeps stakeholders alert and creates a shared vocabulary for the debrief.

---

### 2. Internal Hackathon
**Goal:** Turn research insights into stakeholder-generated solutions.
**When:** After presenting findings, especially when multiple departments are affected.

**Setup:**
- Present research challenges (2-5 specific pain points or friction areas)
- Form cross-functional teams with mixed expertise (product, design, marketing, CS)
- Give teams 1-2 hours to brainstorm, sketch, or prototype solutions
- Each team presents their solution; group votes or discusses feasibility
- Best ideas feed directly into the roadmap or backlog

When stakeholders generate their own solutions from research, they feel ownership over the findings and are far more likely to act on them.

---

### 3. Pin the Pain Point on the Persona
**Goal:** Surface and correct stakeholder assumptions about user pain points.
**When:** When introducing personas, or when you suspect assumptions are misaligned with research.

**Setup:**
- Print large persona posters and hang them (or use Miro/Mural for remote)
- Give each stakeholder Post-its
- Everyone writes down the pain points they believe each persona is experiencing, then places them on the persona
- Debrief by comparing what stakeholders assumed vs. what research actually found
- Discussion surfaces where assumptions were right, wrong, and where gaps exist

---

### 4. UXR Escape Room
**Goal:** Make research findings tangible and memorable through active problem-solving.
**When:** When stakeholders need deeper understanding of findings and cross-team collaboration.

**Setup:**
- Design 4-6 puzzles that represent inefficient workflows or pain points from research
- Each puzzle must be solved to "escape" — solving the puzzle is analogous to solving the real user problem
- Set a 45-minute time limit
- Debrief: connect each puzzle solution to the real product decision it represents

Escape rooms work especially well for complex, multi-step user journeys where the pain is spread across touchpoints.

---

### 5. Insight Auction
**Goal:** Help stakeholders prioritize which research findings deserve the most attention.
**When:** At the end of a research cycle when there are many insights and the team needs to focus.

**Setup:**
- Break research into 8-12 distinct insights
- Give each stakeholder a limited number of tokens or points (e.g., 10)
- Present each insight; stakeholders bid on the ones they believe are most important
- Higher bids = higher perceived priority
- Debrief: discuss why certain insights received high bids and what that means for roadmap decisions

The forced prioritization conversation is often more valuable than the auction itself — it surfaces disagreements between departments about what matters most.

---

### 6. Data Detective
**Goal:** Engage stakeholders in synthesizing qualitative data themselves.
**When:** When you have raw data (quotes, transcripts, behavior patterns) and want stakeholders to build genuine empathy with the findings.

**Setup:**
- Select 15-20 key quotes, data points, or behavior patterns — these are the "clues"
- Form "detective teams" (pattern detectives, behavior analysts, etc.)
- Present a mystery: "Why are users dropping off during checkout?"
- Teams analyze the clues to solve the mystery
- Teams present their findings; researcher synthesizes across teams

Stakeholders who generate their own insights from raw data trust those insights more than ones handed to them in a report.

---

### 7. Persona Showdown
**Goal:** Get stakeholders to actively debate and align on persona prioritization.
**When:** When introducing personas or when teams need to agree on which user type to prioritize for a feature.

**Setup:**
- Create detailed persona cards (motivations, pain points, behaviors, goals)
- Develop 3-5 scenarios where personas have competing needs
- Assign teams to represent specific personas
- Teams advocate for their persona in each scenario; group votes on who "wins"
- Debrief: use results to align on which personas should drive near-term decisions

---

### 8. UXR Time Capsule
**Goal:** Spark long-term thinking about how user needs will evolve.
**When:** During roadmap planning or when working on long-term strategy.

**Setup:**
- Create prediction sheets asking stakeholders to forecast user behavior changes at 1, 5, and 10 years
- Break into groups by persona or product area
- Seal predictions (store in Notion or a shared doc with a locked date)
- Revisit after 6 months or 1 year; compare predictions to actual research findings
- Debrief: what did we get right? What surprised us? What does this tell us about our assumptions?

---

### 9. UXR Jeopardy
**Goal:** Reinforce research findings through competitive, memorable knowledge testing.
**When:** After presenting findings to large or cross-functional groups who need to retain specific insights.

**Setup:**
- Build a Jeopardy board with categories based on research themes (e.g., "User Pain Points," "Behavioral Patterns," "Direct Quotes," "Task Failures")
- Write questions at varying difficulty/point values within each category
- Divide stakeholders into teams
- Teams take turns selecting categories and answering questions
- Debrief: address any wrong answers to correct misunderstandings; reinforce the most critical findings

Best for end-of-research-phase celebrations and making findings feel like shared company knowledge.

---

### 10. Hot Seat Personas
**Goal:** Build empathy by having stakeholders embody user personas in real time.
**When:** At the start of a project when stakeholders are disconnected from users, or when assumptions need challenging.

**Setup:**
- Assign each stakeholder a persona to study in advance
- One stakeholder takes the "hot seat" and must answer questions as if they are the persona
- The group asks questions about how the persona would behave in specific scenarios
- The hot seat participant stays in character
- Rotate through personas; debrief on what the exercise surfaced about each persona's reality

The role-play forces stakeholders to inhabit user frustrations rather than observe them from a distance.

## Remote-specific share-out formats

When there's no pizza to lure people in and no walls to paste personas on, get creative with what remote enables.

**Remote gallery viewing (Miro or Mural):**
- Build a digital gallery with stations: personas, journey maps, top pain points, notable quotes
- Host a live session where attendees vote on which station to visit first
- Use breakout rooms per station — each group explores for 10-15 minutes
- Include an Easter Egg in each breakout room (a surprising insight, a funny clip, a small prize)
- Reconvene for group debrief

**GIF highlights reel:**
- Turn your best video clips into short GIFs showing the same user behavior across multiple participants
- Compile into a highlights reel showing frequency of a behavior pattern
- Share in Slack or embed in Notion — consumable in under 2 minutes

**Investigative article format:**
- Write your research findings as a journalist would — an investigative article or "breaking news" report
- Frame a major pain point as a news story: "BREAKING: 73% of users abandon checkout at step 3"
- Include "eyewitness accounts" (direct user quotes) and "expert analysis" (your recommendations)
- Particularly effective for findings that need executive attention

**User panel (live or async):**
- Invite 3-4 research participants to a live panel with stakeholders
- Stakeholders submit questions in advance; researcher facilitates
- Alternatively: record a 20-minute Q&A with users and share the edited version asynchronously
- Hearing from users directly changes the conversation in ways no report can replicate

**Netflix-style documentary:**
- Record a 30-45 minute documentary (or 3-4 15-minute "episodes") following a day in a user's life
- Use screen recordings, quotes, journey maps, and narration
- Share as episodes with a cliffhanger to drive continued engagement
- Takes more time but has lasting impact — teams reference these months later

**Quarterly user day:**
- Dedicate one full day per quarter to users
- Sessions: most compelling findings of the quarter, AMA with the researcher, user panel, workshop on acting on research
- Treat it as an internal conference — schedule in advance, create an agenda, give it a name
- Even if the full day isn't possible, a 2-hour monthly session covers the essentials

## The insight format for any share-out

Regardless of format, every individual insight should be structured the same way:

**Headline:** Consequence-focused, not behavior-focused (what the behavior means, not just what it is)

**Finding:** What you observed, with frequency or magnitude

**Evidence:** Video clip, direct quote, or screenshot — the most powerful one you have

**Recommendation:** One pointed action, even if it's obvious — give teams a starting point

**Relevant team:** Who owns this, so it doesn't sit in the void

## Checklist before any share-out

- [ ] Do I know who's in the room and what they care about?
- [ ] Is there a TL;DR slide that can be understood in 60 seconds?
- [ ] Does every major finding have a video clip or direct quote as evidence?
- [ ] Does every problem have a recommendation attached?
- [ ] Is the presentation completable in 20-30 minutes?
- [ ] Are there links to deeper materials for those who want more?
- [ ] Does the last slide include concrete next steps with names and dates?
- [ ] Have I included at least one surprising or emotionally resonant moment?

## Output options

When someone triggers this skill:

1. **Presentation structure** — given a set of findings, structure a full research presentation with slides outlined, following the TL;DR + findings + recommendations + next steps format
2. **Insight write-ups** — rewrite flat observations as compelling, consequence-focused insights with evidence and recommendations
3. **Format recommendation** — given a specific stakeholder situation (disengaged team, remote setting, strategic share-out), recommend the best format from the creative options above

5. **Remote share-out plan** — given a research project and a remote team, design a complete engagement plan for sharing findings

## Researcher reminders

- Catering to your audience is not dumbing down your research — it's the entire job
- Show, don't tell: a 60-second video clip of a user struggling does more than three slides of data
- Every finding needs a recommendation, even if it feels obvious — teams need a starting point
- Research that sits in a folder is research that never happened as far as your organization is concerned
- The most memorable share-outs involve the audience — games, workshops, and panels change research from something done to stakeholders into something done with them
- Quantitative data supplements qualitative, it doesn't replace it — but having the analytics number alongside the qualitative finding makes both more credible
- A TL;DR slide is not a cop-out — it's the single most effective thing you can do for executive engagement
- GIFs and video clips are your most powerful communication tools and they're chronically underused
- The goal is not a beautiful report — the goal is decisions that benefit users
