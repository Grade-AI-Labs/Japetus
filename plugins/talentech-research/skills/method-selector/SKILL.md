# Method Selector

This skill helps you choose the right research method — or combination of methods — for any research question. It moves from research goals to buckets to specific methods, and flags questions that research simply cannot answer.

## Start here: the three-step method selection process

Never jump straight to a method. Always start with what you're trying to learn.

### Step 1: Write a research statement

One or two sentences describing what you're trying to understand at a high level.

Model: "We want to better understand how users [think about / make decisions on / interact with] [subject of research / product] in order to [create / improve] [product / website / app / service]."

### Step 2: Define 2-3 research goals

Goals are the specific areas you need to have enough information on by the end of the study to make confident decisions. Maximum 3 — more than that increases scope and makes depth impossible.

**Common generative goals:**
- Discover people's current processes and decision-making about [subject], and how they feel about the overall experience
- Learn about people's current pain points, frustrations, and barriers with [current process / tools] and how they would improve it
- Understand what [subject] means to people — how they define it and why it matters to them

**Common evaluative goals:**
- Evaluate how people are using / interacting with [product / prototype / website / app / service]
- Uncover the current tools people use to [achieve goal] and their experience with those tools
- Learn about pain points and barriers related to [current process / tools]

Note: "Learn about pain points and barriers" sits in both buckets — it can be explored generatively (through open conversation) or evaluatively (by observing someone use the product). Your judgment and context determine which approach fits best.

### Step 3: Pick a primary bucket

**Generative research** — understanding who users are, how they think, what they experience in their lives beyond or alongside a product. Product-agnostic. Exploratory. Produces the "why" behind behavior.

Goals that belong here: current processes / decision-making, pain points (conversational), meaning-making, mental models, motivations.

**Evaluative research** — assessing how a product or concept performs when placed in front of a user. Not just functionality, but findability, efficiency, and emotional response.

Goals that belong here: how people interact with a product, pain points with a specific interface, tool comparison, prototype feedback.

**Hybrid (mixed methods)** — start in one bucket, complement with the other. This is the norm for complex questions. Pick a primary bucket, then layer in methods from the other to triangulate.

## Question-type buckets

Before selecting a method, categorize the research question by what kind of answer it needs.

### The quantitative bucket

Questions that start with or imply: "What...", "How many...", "Can people...", "Which competitors...", "Does this..."

These questions need larger sample sizes because you're trying to generalize to a population. Small samples cannot answer them reliably. Redirect to quantitative methods.

**Example:** "What are the reasons people use our app?" → Survey, not 5 interviews.
**Example:** "Can people navigate our new design?" → Unmoderated tree testing or product analytics, not 5-person usability test presented as representative.

**Methods for quantitative questions:**
- Surveys
- Unmoderated tree testing
- Unmoderated card sorting
- Competitive benchmarking / quantitative usability testing
- Unmoderated usability testing
- Product analytics
- A/B testing

### The qualitative bucket

Questions with "why" — or questions about feelings, attitudes, processes, and mental models. Even if the word "why" isn't in the request, listen for these:
- How do people *feel* about X?
- What are people's *attitudes* toward X?
- What *journey* are people going through?
- How do people *think* about X?

**Methods for qualitative questions:**
- 1x1 generative interviews
- Qualitative usability testing / experience feedback sessions
- Mental model interviews
- Jobs to Be Done interviews
- Journey mapping interviews
- Diary studies
- Contextual inquiry / ethnography / ride-along

### The mixed-methods bucket

"How" questions often signal mixed methods — not because you can't choose one, but because the richest answer comes from both.

Examples:
- "How are people using this feature?" → analytics + qualitative interviews
- "How can we improve the app?" → survey to identify patterns + interviews to understand the why

**Two mixed-methods approaches:**

**Exploratory sequential** — start qualitative, follow with quantitative to confirm or quantify patterns. Good when you don't yet know what to measure.

**Explanatory sequential** — start with quantitative data (analytics, survey), then dive qualitatively into the most significant trends to understand the "why" behind them.

## Questions research cannot answer

This is the most important section. Some of the most common research requests are unanswerable — and accepting them leads to bad data, confused stakeholders, and eroded trust in research.

**"Which design do users prefer?"**
Small sample sizes cannot answer preference reliably. Five people splitting 3-2 on two designs tells you nothing generalizable. Redirect: focus on usability, not preference. Small UI changes → A/B testing.

**"Would people use this feature?"**
Stated future intent does not predict behavior. 51 million Americans have unused gym memberships — they intended to go. Past behavior predicts future behavior; stated intent does not. Redirect: behavioral pilot, analytics on similar features, or JTBD interviews to understand the underlying need.

**"What would people pay for this?"**
Qualitative research with small samples cannot reliably answer willingness-to-pay questions. Redirect: Van Westendorp pricing survey, conjoint analysis, or competitive pricing research.

**"Do people like this design?"**
Opinion-based yes/no questions from small samples are not valid or reliable. People also tend toward socially desirable answers. Redirect: focus on perception and reaction (how they describe it, what it communicates, how it makes them feel) rather than like/dislike.

**"Can you validate our assumptions?"**
Research with small samples explores and evaluates hypotheses — it does not validate them. Redirect: reframe as exploration — "let's understand how users currently experience X" rather than "let's confirm our hypothesis."

**When you can't answer the question as asked, use "No, but..."**
Don't just say no. Name what you can't do, explain why briefly, and offer what you can do instead.
- "We can't determine preference with a small sample, but we can look at usability across both designs and tell you which one users can navigate more successfully."
- "We can't predict future behavior, but we can understand the underlying job they're trying to do and whether this feature addresses it."

## Method reference guide

### Generative methods

**1x1 generative interview** — open-ended conversation exploring experiences, mental models, motivations, and pain points. Best for: understanding the "why" behind behavior, exploring an unknown problem space. Use TEDW question framework throughout.

**Walk-the-store interview** — ask participants to walk you through their last experience with a product or process step by step, screen by screen or moment by moment. Combines elements of contextual inquiry and usability. Best for: understanding real-world usage patterns without a formal task structure.

**Journey mapping interview** — structured exploration of an end-to-end experience across multiple touchpoints, including emotions, pain points, and workarounds at each stage. Best for: understanding the full experience beyond your product.

**Mental model interview** — explores how users mentally organize and understand a concept, process, or domain. Best for: information architecture decisions, feature naming, onboarding design.

**Jobs to be Done (JTBD) interview** — focuses on the job users hire the product to do — the circumstances, motivations, and desired outcomes. Best for: product strategy, feature prioritization, understanding switching behavior.

**Diary study** — participants self-report over time, capturing in-context experiences as they happen. Best for: longitudinal behavior, infrequent events, understanding context that can't be recreated in a lab.

**Contextual inquiry / ethnographic observation / ride-along** — observe users in their natural environment doing real tasks. Best for: uncovering workarounds and behaviors people don't report in interviews.

### Evaluative methods

**Qualitative usability test** — structured feedback session on a mid-fidelity design using TEDW-based questions screen by screen. Not measuring performance — gathering directional feedback. Best for: mid-fidelity designs not yet ready for metric measurement.

**Quantitative usability test** — performance measurement using tasks, metrics (task success, time on task, SEQ, SUS), and a stoplight report. Best for: live products or high-fidelity prototypes where the team needs evidence of usability or benchmarking data.

**Concept testing** — exposing participants to an early-stage concept (low-fidelity) to understand how it aligns with their mental models and current needs. Best for: before designs are built out, when the team still needs to understand if they're solving the right problem.

**Card sorting** — participants organize topics or items into groups that make sense to them. Best for: information architecture, navigation labels, taxonomy design.
- Open card sort: participants create their own categories
- Closed card sort: participants sort into predefined categories
- Sample size: 20-30 per segment

**Tree testing** — participants find items in a text-only site structure without visual design cues. Best for: validating information architecture decisions after card sorting.
- Sample size: 25-30 per segment (unmoderated)

**First-click testing** — measures where users click first when trying to complete a task. Best for: validating navigation placement and page hierarchy.
- Sample size: 25-30 per segment (unmoderated)

**Comparative usability testing** — testing 2-3 different designs against the same tasks to compare effectiveness and efficiency (not preference). Rotate design order across participants. Best for: when the team has multiple design directions and needs to know which one lets users complete tasks more successfully. Cap at 3 designs maximum.

**Heuristic evaluation** — internal expert review of a product against established usability principles. Best for: quick issues identification when participant access isn't possible.

### Quantitative / survey methods

**Survey** — large-scale data collection on attitudes, behaviors, and demographics. Best for: quantifying patterns found in qualitative research, or understanding prevalence of a known issue.

**True intent study** — intercept survey deployed on the live product (exit pop-up, hover trigger) to capture who is visiting and whether they accomplished their goals. Best for: quickly collecting data on in-product behavior without recruitment. Needs high traffic volume; keep to 1-3 questions.

**NPS (Net Promoter Score)** — measures loyalty and recommendation likelihood. Best used as a tracking metric, not a standalone research method. Always pair with follow-up qualitative for the "why."

**Benchmarking** — repeated measurement of the same metrics over time (SUS, task success, time on task) to demonstrate improvement or decline. Best for: proving the ROI of design changes.

### Less-common but powerful methods

**FIDO (Freehand Interactive Design Online)** — participants build their ideal design from components you provide before prototypes exist. Created by Fidelity. Best for: participatory design, early-stage concept exploration, understanding what components people find useful or confusing before building. Analyze by: most popular components, percentage of competitor elements chosen, similarities and differences across participants.

**Run of Post** — examine all off-platform communication sent to a specific user group over a set period to find inconsistencies, gaps, and missed opportunities. Created by Caroline Jarrett. Best for: understanding communication strategy fragmentation; can also be used to understand how on-platform behavior connects to communication. Choose one specific user group, gather 2 weeks to 6 months of communication depending on frequency, and analyze for narrative coherence and gaps.

**Cloze test** — remove every fifth word from a 250-word text sample and ask participants to fill in the blanks. Measures content comprehension. Score: number correct / total blanks. Benchmarks: 60%+ = text is understood; 40-60% = difficulty likely; below 40% = needs significant revision. Best for: legal, healthcare, or complex instructional copy; any content where comprehension is critical.

**Five-second test** — show a design for five seconds, then ask what they remember. Best for: measuring whether a design communicates its core message immediately. Questions: "What do you think this website was about?", "What product or service does it offer?", "What was the most prominent element?"

**Aesthetic explanation** — ask participants to describe initial impressions of a design in their own words. Not "do you like it?" but "how would you describe it?" Best for: understanding how a brand or design lands emotionally and perceptually.

**Open / closed word choice** — show participants a design and ask them to select words that describe it (closed: from a pre-defined list; open: their own words). Best for: understanding whether a design communicates intended brand attributes.

**Visual quality ranking** — participants rate a design against 3-5 specific brand attributes on a 1-5 scale. Best for: after open/closed word choice establishes which attributes resonate, to quantify their strength.

## Visual testing: important caveats

Visual testing does NOT answer:
- Whether one design is "better" than another
- Which design users prefer
- Whether users like the colors
- Whether users like the design
- The impact of minimal visual changes (use A/B testing for that)

Visual testing DOES tell you:
- Initial reactions and impressions of a design or brand
- How people perceive and describe brand attributes
- How visuals impact navigation behavior and task completion

Always run usability testing before visual testing. No one will appreciate a beautiful product that doesn't work. The hierarchy: functional → reliable → usable → delightful. Aesthetics live at the top. Don't invest in them until the foundation is solid.

## Mixing methods: recommendations

Use 2-3 methods per project, not one. The most common and productive combinations:

- **Generative interviews + survey** — interviews uncover the "why," survey quantifies how widespread the patterns are
- **Walk-the-store interviews + journey mapping exercise** — walks capture the step-by-step, mapping captures the emotional arc
- **Qualitative usability test + ideation workshop** — feedback session informs the design direction, workshop activates it
- **Quantitative usability test + debrief interview** — metrics show what broke, follow-up questions explain why
- **Analytics / survey + qualitative interviews** — data shows where the problem is, interviews explain why it's happening (explanatory sequential)
- **Generative interviews + concept test** — understand the problem space first, then validate early direction

When choosing combinations: pick a primary method that directly answers your research goals, then layer in a secondary method that either quantifies qualitative patterns or adds context to quantitative data.

## Output format

When someone triggers this skill, produce one or more of:

1. **Method recommendation** — primary method + rationale + optional complementary method
2. **"No, but..." redirect** — if the question is unanswerable, name what you can't do and offer what you can
3. **Method comparison** — if choosing between two methods, lay out what each answers and doesn't answer
4. **Full method plan** — research statement → goals → bucket → primary method → secondary method → rationale

## Researcher reminders

- Always start with research goals before choosing a method — the method serves the goals, not the other way around
- Small sample sizes cannot answer "what," "how many," or "which do people prefer" questions — redirect to quantitative methods
- "Can you validate our assumptions?" is not a research question — explore and evaluate, never validate
- Mixing methods is the norm for complex questions — don't force everything into one methodology
- Usability always before visual testing — beautiful products that don't work still fail
- "No, but..." is one of the most powerful tools in a researcher's kit — use it confidently and always pair the no with an alternative path
