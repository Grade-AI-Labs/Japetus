# Ethics Checklist

This skill reviews research projects against ethical best practices — covering informed consent, participant wellbeing, data privacy, sensitive topics, vulnerable populations, and researcher responsibilities. Based on APA, ESOMAR, BPS, and ACM frameworks, adapted for applied UXR practice.

## Why ethics matters in UXR

Research ethics isn't bureaucracy. It's how we protect participants from harm, maintain the trust that makes research possible, and produce findings that are credible and defensible. When ethical standards slip — even with good intentions — participants can be deceived, harmed, or exploited. Data can be misused. Organizations can face legal liability. And the entire case for user research as a discipline weakens.

Ethics also protects researchers. A clear ethical framework helps you push back when stakeholders ask for something that crosses a line — not because you're being difficult, but because you can name exactly which principle is at stake.

## Domain 1: Informed consent

Informed consent is the cornerstone of ethical research. Participants must understand what they're agreeing to before they agree to it — not after.

**Checklist:**

- [ ] Participants are told the purpose of the research in plain, accessible language
- [ ] Participants are told how long the session will take
- [ ] Participants are told what will happen during the session (activities, tasks, questions)
- [ ] Participants are told how their data will be used and who will see it
- [ ] Participants are told how long their data will be stored
- [ ] Participants are told whether sessions will be recorded (audio, video, screen)
- [ ] Participants are told they can withdraw at any time without penalty
- [ ] Participants are told whether their responses will be kept confidential or anonymous — and the difference between these is made clear
- [ ] Consent is obtained before the session begins, not during or after
- [ ] Consent is documented (written form, digital checkbox, or recorded verbal consent)
- [ ] Children and legally incapacitated adults have proxy consent from a parent or guardian
- [ ] The consent form uses plain language (aim for Grade 8 reading level or below)
- [ ] Participants are given a copy of the consent form to keep

**Confidential vs. anonymous — know the difference:**
Confidential means the researcher knows who said what, but won't share it with others in identifiable form. Anonymous means even the researcher cannot link responses to individuals. Most UXR sessions are confidential, not anonymous — be honest about this.

**Consent form minimum content:**
1. Study title and purpose (plain language)
2. What participation involves
3. Duration
4. How data will be used and stored
5. Who will have access
6. Participant rights (withdraw at any time, no penalty)
7. Contact information for questions
8. Signature / date field

## Domain 2: Participant rights and wellbeing

Participants are not means to an end. They are people doing you a favor. Their wellbeing takes priority over data quality.

**Checklist:**

- [ ] Participants can withdraw at any time without penalty — and this is stated explicitly at the start of every session, not buried in the consent form
- [ ] Participants are not pressured to answer questions they find uncomfortable
- [ ] The session does not contain tasks or questions that could cause psychological distress without a prior warning and an opt-out mechanism
- [ ] Participants are debriefed at the end of the session — they know what the research is for and what happens next
- [ ] Incentives are appropriate to the task but not so high they create undue inducement (pressure to participate against their better judgment)
- [ ] Participants are not deceived about the purpose of the research unless deception is scientifically necessary AND approved by a review process AND they are fully debriefed afterward
- [ ] No participant is recorded without explicit consent
- [ ] Participants are thanked for their time and treated with respect throughout
- [ ] If distress occurs during a session, there is a clear plan for how to respond (pause, offer support, allow withdrawal)
- [ ] Physical sessions are conducted in a safe, accessible environment

**On incentives:** Incentives should reflect the time and effort asked of participants — not be so high that people participate despite concerns about the study or their own wellbeing. A useful rule: would a reasonable person accept this incentive without feeling coerced? If yes, it's appropriate.

**On deception:** Deception is sometimes justified in research (e.g., not revealing the full hypothesis to prevent demand characteristics). When used:
- The deception must be necessary — no way to answer the research question without it
- Participants must be fully debriefed immediately after the session
- Participants must have the opportunity to withdraw their data after the debrief
- The benefit to knowledge must outweigh the harm of the deception

## Domain 3: Data privacy and security

Every piece of participant data you collect is a trust relationship. Treat it accordingly.

**Checklist:**

**Data collection:**
- [ ] Only collecting data that is necessary for the research goals (data minimization)
- [ ] Participants know exactly what data is being collected
- [ ] No collection of sensitive personal data (health, financial, racial/ethnic origin, religious beliefs, sexual orientation) without explicit additional consent
- [ ] If biometric data is involved (eye tracking, physiological measures), specific consent has been obtained

**Data storage:**
- [ ] Data is stored securely with appropriate access controls
- [ ] Personal identifiers (names, email addresses, faces in video) are separated from research data where possible
- [ ] Data is stored for only as long as necessary, with a defined retention period communicated to participants
- [ ] A data retention and deletion schedule exists and is followed
- [ ] Cloud storage complies with applicable data protection regulations (GDPR in EU/UK, CCPA in California, etc.)

**Data sharing:**
- [ ] Research data is only shared with people named in the consent form
- [ ] Quotes, clips, and findings are anonymized or pseudonymized before sharing externally
- [ ] No personally identifiable information appears in reports, presentations, or repositories without explicit consent
- [ ] Video clips shared with stakeholders have had identifiable details reviewed (faces, names mentioned, location details)
- [ ] Participant data is never sold or shared with third parties without explicit consent

**GDPR and data protection basics (EU/UK):**
- You must have a lawful basis for processing personal data (consent is one basis; legitimate interests is another)
- Participants have the right to access their data, correct it, and request its deletion
- Data transfers outside the EU/UK require adequate protections
- If operating under GDPR: document your lawful basis, retention period, and data flows

## Domain 4: Research design integrity

Ethical research is not just about how you treat participants — it's about how honestly and rigorously you conduct and report the work.

**Checklist:**

**Study design:**
- [ ] Research questions are genuinely open — the study is designed to learn, not to confirm a predetermined conclusion
- [ ] Methodology is appropriate for the research questions (not chosen because it produces the "right" answer)
- [ ] Sample is recruited to be representative of the population of interest — not cherry-picked to confirm a hypothesis
- [ ] Research is not designed to manipulate or deceive participants into behaviors that benefit the organization at their expense

**Reporting:**
- [ ] Findings are reported honestly, including findings that contradict expectations or stakeholder preferences
- [ ] Limitations of the study are acknowledged (sample size, recruitment constraints, methodological tradeoffs)
- [ ] Quotes and data are not taken out of context to support a predetermined narrative
- [ ] Negative findings are not suppressed
- [ ] Uncertainty is acknowledged — small qualitative samples are presented as directional, not statistically generalizable
- [ ] Data is not fabricated, manipulated, or selectively reported

**Conflicts of interest:**
- [ ] Any conflicts of interest (financial, personal, organizational) that could bias the research are disclosed
- [ ] Research conducted by an interested party (e.g., the product team testing their own product) is clearly framed with that limitation acknowledged

## Domain 5: Vulnerable populations

Extra care is required when research involves people who may be less able to protect their own interests or who may be at heightened risk of harm.

**Vulnerable population groups include (but are not limited to):**
- Children and young people (under 18)
- People with cognitive, intellectual, or developmental disabilities
- People with mental health conditions
- Older adults with diminished capacity
- People in crisis or acute distress
- Pregnant participants (depending on research topic)
- People in economically precarious situations (undue inducement risk)
- People in dependent relationships with the researcher or organization (employees, students, patients)
- Participants from marginalized or stigmatized communities

**Checklist for vulnerable participants:**

- [ ] A genuine rationale exists for involving vulnerable participants — if the research question can be answered with a non-vulnerable population, it should be
- [ ] For children: written parental or guardian consent obtained; child's assent also sought where developmentally appropriate
- [ ] Capacity to consent has been assessed where relevant — does this person understand what they're agreeing to?
- [ ] Session length, task complexity, and question sensitivity are appropriate for the population
- [ ] Researcher has relevant training or experience working with this population, or is supervised by someone who does
- [ ] Safety plan exists for distress: if a participant becomes upset or discloses a safeguarding concern, you know what to do
- [ ] Safeguarding obligations are clear: researchers have a duty to act if a participant discloses harm to themselves or others
- [ ] Incentives do not create undue inducement for people in financially vulnerable circumstances
- [ ] Findings about marginalized communities are reported with care — consider how the language used could stigmatize or harm

**On safeguarding disclosures:** If a participant discloses abuse, self-harm, suicidal ideation, or risk to others during a session, confidentiality does not override your duty of care. Know your organization's safeguarding policy before you begin research with vulnerable populations.

## Domain 6: Researcher responsibilities

Ethics doesn't end with participant care. Researchers have responsibilities to their organizations, their discipline, and themselves.

**Checklist:**

- [ ] Research is only conducted within your competence — if a topic or population is outside your expertise, seek supervision or support
- [ ] You are not conducting research under conditions that would compromise its integrity (impossible timelines, pressure to produce specific findings)
- [ ] You can articulate a clear ethical justification for the research — it serves users, not just the organization
- [ ] You have a process for pushing back when asked to conduct unethical research (leading questions, deceptive framing, pressure to suppress findings)
- [ ] Participant data is handled with the same care you would want your own data handled with
- [ ] You are taking care of your own wellbeing — especially when research involves distressing topics, trauma, or vicarious exposure to difficult content

**Researcher wellbeing:** Research on sensitive topics — end-of-life care, mental health, domestic violence, financial distress — takes a toll on researchers too. Build in debrief time for yourself. Acknowledge when research is emotionally heavy. Secondary traumatic stress is real.

## The debrief

Every research session should end with a debrief — a brief explanation to participants of what the research is for and what happens next. This closes the loop, respects participants' intelligence, and is required when any form of deception was used.

**Minimum debrief content:**
- The purpose of the research (in plain language)
- How their participation helps
- What will happen with the data
- When or where findings might be published or shared (if applicable)
- Who to contact with questions
- A genuine thank-you

**Sample debrief script:**
"That's the end of the session — thank you so much for your time. Just to give you a bit of context: we're using this research to [purpose, e.g., understand how people navigate our onboarding experience]. Your input will help us identify areas to improve. Your responses will be kept confidential — we won't identify you by name in any reports. We'll store the recording for [X months] and then delete it. If you have any questions about the research or your data, you can reach us at [contact]. Do you have any questions for me?"

## Sensitive topic checklist

Some research topics require additional ethical care regardless of the participant population.

Topics requiring extra ethical planning:
- Mental health, suicide, self-harm
- Domestic violence, abuse, trauma
- Financial distress, debt, poverty
- Substance use or addiction
- Serious illness, end-of-life care, bereavement
- Sexuality and gender identity
- Political beliefs in high-risk contexts
- Immigration status
- Experiences of discrimination or racism
- Criminal history

**For sensitive topics:**
- [ ] Consent form explicitly mentions the sensitive nature of the topic
- [ ] Participants are given an explicit opt-out from specific questions without penalty
- [ ] A resource list is prepared in case participants need support (crisis lines, relevant services)
- [ ] The researcher has considered how to respond if a participant becomes distressed
- [ ] Findings are written and shared with care — consider how language choices could harm or stigmatize communities
- [ ] Consider whether remote research (which provides more participant privacy) is preferable to in-person for this topic

## Pre-study ethics audit

Before beginning any study, run this quick audit:

**1. Is the research necessary?**
Could the question be answered using existing data, analytics, or past research? If yes — use that first.

**2. Is the research design honest?**
Is this study genuinely designed to learn, or to confirm a decision already made?

**3. Are participants protected?**
Do they understand what they're agreeing to? Can they withdraw? Is their data secure?

**4. Are there any vulnerable participants?**
If yes — is there a genuine reason to include them? Are there additional protections in place?

**5. Is the topic sensitive?**
If yes — are there content warnings, opt-outs, and a researcher support plan?

**6. Can the findings be reported honestly?**
Are you in a position to share findings that contradict stakeholder preferences?

If any answer raises a concern — address it before recruiting begins.

## Red flags: when to stop and seek guidance

- A stakeholder wants you to design questions that steer participants toward a predetermined answer
- You're being asked to suppress or not report findings that don't support the business case
- Participants are being recruited from a dependent population (employees, customers in a service relationship) without additional safeguards
- The research involves a sensitive topic and there's no support plan if a participant discloses distress
- You're being asked to collect more personal data than is necessary for the research goals
- A participant appears distressed during a session and there's pressure to continue
- Findings are being presented to executives without the limitations being communicated

In any of these situations: pause, name the concern, and escalate if needed. Your professional integrity is the foundation of everything your research is worth.

## Researcher reminders

- Informed consent is the floor, not the ceiling — ethical practice goes well beyond getting a signature
- Confidential and anonymous are not the same thing — be honest with participants about which applies
- Data minimization is an ethical principle, not just a legal one — only collect what you actually need
- Negative findings are ethically required to report — suppression is a form of research misconduct
- Sensitive topics require a researcher wellbeing plan too — secondary trauma is real
- If a participant discloses harm during a session, confidentiality does not override duty of care
- When a stakeholder asks for something that crosses an ethical line, you need language to say so — not just a feeling that something is wrong
- Ethics review isn't a one-time checkbox — it's an ongoing responsibility throughout the research process
